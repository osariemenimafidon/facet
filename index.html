A compression-ignition engine is certified on one fuel and then operated, for thousands of hours, on whatever the market supplies. The fuels now sold into the same tank no longer share a property envelope.

Why density is not a detail

Fuel injection systems meter volume. The mass actually delivered per injection scales with density, so a fuel outside the calibrated envelope produces a fuelling error the control system has no reason to know about. Two further properties propagate into the same combustion event:

PropertyMechanismConsequence
Density Injection is volumetric; delivered mass scales with density Fuelling error — torque and emissions shift together
Bulk modulus Governs pressure-wave propagation through line and rail Injection timing shifts; documented for biodiesel in pump-line-nozzle systems
Cetane number Sets ignition delay Premixed and diffusion burn fractions change; NOx and PM move in opposition

The ignition-delay effect is not a constant a calibrator can subtract. Reported differences between B7 and neat HVO run to roughly 2.5 ms at 550 °C, narrowing to about 0.7 ms at 650 °C — the error is itself temperature-dependent.

And the margin is closing

CARB's draft Tier 5 off-road proposal, released February 2026, would cut NOx by up to 90% and PM by up to 75% against current EPA Tier 4 final standards, phasing in across 2031–2036. Standards set that close to the floor leave very little headroom for an uncompensated fuel-property shift. The proposal is not yet adopted.

The two questions

Q1 · Technical

Can injection control adapt to the fuel it is actually burning?

Closely enough to close the certified-to-in-service gap — and what must it sense to do so? Direct property measurement, inference from combustion feedback, or a declared blend input?

Q2 · Methodological

What evidence would let such control reach production?

And under what criteria can verification evidence generated for one engine program be legitimately reused for another? Adaptive-control concepts are not scarce in the literature. What is scarce is an accepted account of what evidence is sufficient, and of when cross-program reuse is defensible rather than merely convenient. The barrier between a working concept and a production calibration is the cost and structure of verification evidence, not the novelty of the control law.

The program's centre of gravity

Method commitments

Constraints accepted in advance, not aspirations.

  1. Open federal data only. Every result must be reproducible by someone with no access to proprietary engine data, test cells, or manufacturer cooperation.
  2. Provenance at every fetch. Source URL, retrieval date, byte count and SHA-256 are logged before a file is used.
  3. No number is typed by hand. Every figure in every document is interpolated from a statistics file the pipeline writes, so prose cannot drift from data.
  4. A verification gate precedes publication. Nothing is published until the investigator has personally reproduced the pipeline and spot-checked outputs against the primary source.
  5. Findings are published whether or not they are favourable — including negative results and defects found in public data.
  6. Self-funded, no sponsor. There is no party whose interests a result could be shaped to serve.

What would make this program wrong

Stated in advance, so it can fail honestly.

  • If in-service fuel property variation proves small relative to existing calibration tolerance, the motivating premise collapses and the program should say so.
  • If adaptive control turns out to be routinely deployed already and simply undocumented in public sources, the contribution reduces to documentation.
  • If cross-program evidence reuse proves unformalisable — every engine program genuinely a special case — then Q2 has a negative answer, which is itself worth publishing.
